# Founder Design Interview

## Purpose

Close the brainstorming stage through a structured conversation between the
founder and Game Director.

Each round records approved decisions, explicit changes, and unresolved
questions. Later agents must treat approved answers as product requirements, not
casual suggestions.

## Approved global settings

KillWeb exposes five independent settings:

1. Opponent competence.
2. National challenge.
3. Command assistance.
4. Historical behavior.
5. Pause policy.

This prevents accessibility, AI strength, historical behavior, and starting
country difficulty from becoming one incoherent selector.

## Round 01: Player identity and continuity

Status: approved.

### Approved decisions

1. The player controls the enduring strategic will of a country, not one
   particular leader.
2. Elections change leaders, coalitions, policies, legitimacy, and institutional
   behavior without normally ending the campaign.
3. The player continues through an incoming government after losing an election.
4. Coups, revolutions, and constitutional crises may replace the regime and
   create breakaway regions, disloyal institutions, or fragmented forces.
5. During civil war, the player chooses which claimant represents their
   continuation.
6. Occupation or annexation may continue through resistance, government in
   exile, collaboration, or a successor movement.
7. The player may intentionally reform constitutions, institutions, territorial
   organization, civil military relations, and the relationship between religion
   and the state.
8. Companies, citizens, parties, commanders, and institutions retain limited
   autonomy.
9. Morally reckless strategies remain possible, but every relevant system may
   react.
10. National collapse does not automatically end the campaign.

### Political actor addendum

Status: approved.

1. The player selects a country and begins through its current administration.
   The current leader is the initial political vessel for the national campaign.
2. Every playable country begins with at least twenty sourced political actors.
   Major powers and politically complex states may require substantially more.
3. The actor set may include current leaders, plausible successors, cabinet
   members, governing and opposition figures, regional leaders, military and
   security leaders, royal or religious figures, technocrats, civil society
   figures, and emerging leaders.
4. Actors exist inside a power network containing parties, factions,
   constituencies, relationships, institutional positions, policy preferences,
   eligibility, public support, and elite support.
5. Elections produce plausible candidates through party processes, polling,
   eligibility, coalitions, finance, scandals, performance, and the alternate
   history already created by the simulation.
6. A coup must originate in a simulated institution or coalition. Its public
   leader must be plausible from that actor's documented role and relationships.
   The game must never present an unsupported claim that a living person secretly
   intends to conduct a coup.
7. Cabinet appointments and senior commands are gameplay decisions because
   competence, ideology, loyalty, corruption, institutional trust, and personal
   relationships affect implementation.
8. Political actors may rise, retire, lose office, defect, become imprisoned,
   die, reconcile, form coalitions, or become associated with new movements.
9. The default campaign follows the country through succession. During a severe
   regime crisis, the player may choose to follow a displaced government,
   breakaway authority, or other claimant.
10. Real political actors anchor the opening years. Procedurally generated future
    actors gradually enter as the timeline moves beyond a defensible roster of
    real people.

### Derived geography requirement

Every country needs province level strategic geography comparable in function to
Hearts of Iron IV.

The province layer must support:

1. Territorial control.
2. Occupation.
3. Breakaway regions.
4. Civil wars.
5. Fronts and theaters.
6. Population and political geography.
7. Economy and resources.
8. Infrastructure and logistics.
9. Dynamic borders.

Exact province topology remains an open world model question.

## Round 02: World time, autonomy, and delegation

Status: approved.

### Approved decisions

1. The world uses one continuous global clock.
2. The practical clock modes are paused, tactical, operational, campaign, and
   strategic. Exact rates will be chosen through prototype testing.
3. Tactical time supports detailed missions. Strategic time advances days or
   weeks. Every mode advances the same deterministic simulation.
4. Critical events may slow or pause time according to the selected pause policy.
5. Existing policies, construction, research, procurement, missions, trade, and
   diplomatic commitments continue without new player input.
6. Demographics, markets, companies, public opinion, institutions, and technology
   diffusion continue autonomously.
7. A country without new priorities continues its inherited trajectory. It does
   not freeze, but it loses opportunities and may stagnate relative to active
   rivals.
8. A single player campaign does not advance while the game is closed.
9. The player may directly control or delegate any major portfolio, including the
   economy, research, infrastructure, procurement, diplomacy, intelligence,
   domestic government, military theaters, formations, and routine missions.
10. Delegation is governed through intent. The player sets an objective, budget,
    risk tolerance, political boundaries, geographic scope, and review triggers.
11. Staff operate under the same rules and resources as the player. Their quality
    depends on institutions, personnel, doctrine, information, corruption,
    loyalty, and workload.
12. Staff must explain what they did, which resources they used, and which
    assumptions guided the decision.
13. The player may resume direct control at any time, but committed actions,
    contractual obligations, movement, and communication delays remain real.
14. KillWeb has no artificial attention currency. Limits emerge from
    administrative capacity, political capital, command bandwidth, budgets,
    personnel, conflicting orders, and institutional friction.
15. Decisions may have deadlines. Pausing stops the deadline clock. If a decision
    expires, the interface must display and apply the governing default.
16. Events use four attention levels: information, recommendation, decision, and
    existential. The pause policy determines how each level behaves.
17. Historical behavior is a preference expressed through current incentives and
    political beliefs. Historical events are never forced after their causal
    prerequisites have disappeared.
18. Simultaneous crises remain possible. Staff triage them, the interface groups
    them, and the player decides which ones deserve direct attention.
19. An audit timeline records decisions, delegation, assumptions, orders,
    resource commitments, and outcomes.
20. The easiest command assistance prevents idle capacity, recommends sensible
    priorities, and pauses for major decisions. It does not create hidden
    resources or guarantee success.

### Delegation fantasy

Delegation is not merely an accessibility feature. It lets the player choose what
kind of game they want to play at a given moment. A player may directly command a
military theater while delegating the economy, then later automate routine
military activity and personally redesign industrial policy. The simulation
continues to connect both portfolios regardless of who currently controls them.

## Round 03: National gameplay loop

Status: approved.

### Core loop

1. Notice a pressure or opportunity.
2. Decide what outcome matters.
3. Choose a plan.
4. Commit authority and resources.
5. Advance time and observe execution.
6. Intervene, delegate, expand, or adapt.

The same loop applies to economic development, political reform, diplomacy,
information activity, intelligence, military operations, and detailed kill
chains.

### Approved decisions

1. The player governs primarily through objectives that describe desired
   outcomes.
2. Objectives are contextual and flexible, not a rigid historical focus tree.
3. National priorities use a soft institutional limit. Excessive commitments
   create competition, delay, conflicting orders, weak oversight, and poor
   coordination.
4. Strategic activity uses four connected levels: objective, program, project or
   operation, and task.
5. Institutions develop competing plans with visible costs, requirements,
   dependencies, risks, uncertainty, consequences, and opportunity costs.
6. KillWeb does not use a universal mana resource. Capacity emerges from money,
   credit, support, administration, industry, labor, expertise, energy,
   materials, logistics, intelligence, diplomacy, readiness, equipment, and
   stockpiles.
7. Resources are represented as understandable stocks, flows, and bottlenecks.
8. The world map remains the primary interface for objectives, projects, units,
   politics, trade, infrastructure, industry, and intelligence.
9. Zoom changes the available decision scale. Strategic objects resolve into
   formations, facilities, networks, and tasks when greater detail is useful.
10. Policies have implementation time, institutional response, and consequences,
    but routine administration and individual contract management are abstracted
    by default.
11. Economic control reflects the actual structure of the country. Market states
    rely more on incentives, procurement, regulation, credit, and emergency
    authority. Command states provide more direct control while introducing
    their own information and efficiency problems.
12. Important companies, facilities, networks, and institutions may exist as
    individual strategic entities. Ordinary activity remains aggregated by
    sector, province, and market.
13. Peacetime is fully playable through development, science, culture, religion,
    infrastructure, political reform, regional integration, energy, and other
    national ambitions.
14. Standing instructions let the player automate recurring responses and define
    conditions for review.
15. National objectives connect naturally to theater plans, operations, mission
    packages, sensor tasking, targeting, engagement, and assessment.
16. The interface controls interruption volume and groups related crisis
    decisions.
17. Every important outcome provides a causal explanation.
18. Complexity is progressively revealed during play. Expert players may expose
    every control immediately.

### Abstraction and fantasy rule

Administrative friction should shape outcomes without turning KillWeb into a
paperwork simulator.

The player acts at the level where a choice creates strategy or fantasy.
Routine allocations, individual procurement contracts, local forms, and mundane
implementation are handled by staff unless the player deliberately opens a more
detailed management view.

A player may choose to manage a granular portfolio, but the game must never
require bureaucratic work merely to prove that the simulation is detailed.
Delegation should compress implementation into clear intent, projected effects,
important tradeoffs, and exceptional decisions.

### Modern strategic entity clarification

The strategic entity model includes traditional Hearts of Iron IV era assets
such as shipyards, steel plants, aircraft factories, arsenals, ports, rail
junctions, and energy facilities.

It also includes modern sources of national power where relevant:

1. Semiconductor designers and fabrication plants.
2. Artificial intelligence laboratories and compute providers.
3. Cloud platforms and data centers.
4. Social media and mass communication platforms.
5. Telecommunications and satellite operators.
6. Battery, drone, robotics, and advanced manufacturing companies.
7. Critical software, cyber security, and payment infrastructure.
8. Pharmaceutical and biotechnology capacity.
9. Rare material processors and advanced machine tool producers.
10. Commercial firms whose production can be converted during mobilization.

Examples may include Meta, TikTok, OpenAI, Anthropic, Kimi, DeepSeek, and their
future equivalents. Their influence depends on ownership, jurisdiction,
leadership, technical capacity, users, data, government relationships, and the
alternate history produced by the campaign.
